# For Syriacists

*What is here, what has not been checked, and six specific things a reader who knows Syriac
could settle in an afternoon.*

## The short version

Everything on this site was read off public-domain plates by one person who reads Latin and
Greek — not Syriac — working with a language model, against standard reference grammar. Where
a paradigm could be collated against Nöldeke it has been, cell by cell, and the collation is
recorded in the record with its disagreements. Where it could not, the reading stands as
proposed and says so.

**No Syriacist has ruled on any of it.** The registry carries the seat and leaves it empty:
`expert = ""`. That is not a disclaimer at the foot of the page; it is the reason for the page.

The asks below are deliberately narrow. None of them is "would you like to review a website."
Each is a specific question with a specific record behind it, most answerable by someone who
knows the language in the time it takes to read this page. They are in order of what would
help most.

## 1. Twenty-seven dictionary entries that need only vowel points

The highest-value item by a distance, because it is not re-extraction — it is pointing.

Twenty-seven entries were read from Payne Smith's 1903 *Compendious Syriac Dictionary* to fill
vocabulary gaps the lessons opened. For each one the consonantal skeleton, the part of speech
and the English gloss were read directly off the plate and are reliable. **The vocalisation was
deliberately left out.** Payne Smith's plates are pointed and the points are visible at the
resolutions used, but this project has no control table for that typeface's pointing and no
second reader, and the standing rule here is never to read diacritics by eye and call them
data. So `voc` is empty in all twenty-seven, on purpose, and each record says so on its face.

A reader who knows Syriac would be adding vowel points to twenty-seven skeletons that are
already correct. Examples: [ܐܒܝܠܐ *mourner*](/sources/r4/p002-abila),
[ܩܕܫ *sanctify*](/sources/r4/p491-qadesh), [ܬܗܘܡܐ *the deep*](/sources/r4/p605-tehoma). The
full shard is on [the sources shelf](/sources).

Ten of the twenty-seven also carry an uncertainty flag, and one of those is a plain coverage
hole rather than a hard question: [ܫܒܩ *forgive*](/sources/r4/p557-shbaq) was cropped from
sense f) onward, so senses a)–e) and the entry's opening line were never read. That one needs
one more look at a page already fetched, not expertise.

## 2. A noun that takes the wrong plural

In [the noun-states survey](/sources/r1/p031-1), row I.b, a word glossed 'foot' is printed with
the **masculine** plural pattern (־ܝܢ / ־ܝ / ־ܐ) where Nöldeke's general rule at §70 predicts
the feminine endings for a feminine noun of that shape.

The obvious explanation — that we misread the plate — has been ruled out: the row was
re-verified on a second, tighter crop and the masculine pattern is genuinely what Nestle
prints. So the remaining question is lexical, and it is beyond us. Is the lexeme
misidentified? Or does Nestle really print it that way, and if so is it his slip or a real
usage? This is flagged in the record rather than quietly "corrected," because correcting it on
the strength of a memorised paradigm is exactly the failure this project is trying not to
commit.

## 3. Seven worked forms with proposed person-assignments

Nestle's §31 states the noun-plus-possessive-suffix rule and follows it with seven worked
example forms, unlabeled. Nöldeke gives the same rule at §145 with a fully labeled ten-item
example set, so the seven were mapped onto his named set by shape and length correlation and
tagged accordingly — [and the record marks every one of those tags proposed, not
proven](/sources/r1/p034-1).

They are plausible. They are not read glyph by glyph at a resolution that would settle them.
Someone who can simply *look* at the seven forms and name the persons would close this in a
minute.

## 4. One word: teth, or taw?

Nestle's preposition list prints **two distinct words** before a single shared gloss 'behind,
after' — which the first pass recorded as one word covering both senses, and which a check
against Nöldeke caught. Re-cropped at 5×, the plate does show two words, and the record now
carries [both of them separately](/sources/r1/p063-1).

What is still open is the second word's exact spelling: at that type size it is visually very
close to the first, and the distinction turns on a **teth against a taw**. Recorded as
uncertain rather than picked.

## 5. Four dot-glosses on the hand track

The [manuscript track](https://paleography.app/syriac) — a pocket Gospel book written at Vienna
in 1554, the copy from which the first book printed in Syriac was set — explains its
pointing to learners in four short glosses: **seyame**, **the dot above**, **the dot below**,
and **the abbreviation mark**. Between them they carry most of the grammar a reader of that
unpointed hand has to recover, and they are the notes a beginner will actually read.

All four are marked `proposed`. Each states counts drawn from the manuscript itself — the dot
above marking the participle 167 times against 79 for the dot below, seyame falling on 1,182 of
2,475 lines, 675 word-forms out of 7,035 appearing in more than one pointing. The counts we
stand behind. The grammatical explanations wrapped around them are the part that wants a
Syriacist's eye.

## 6. Forty-four unkeyed pages

Nestle's chrestomathy runs past Genesis into the *Vitae Prophetarum* and the *Historia
inventionis*. Sixty-six pages are captured as records; twenty-two — the pages the lessons are
built from — are fully keyed and independently diffed against a second printed edition. **The
other forty-four are partially or wholly unkeyed.**

That is a different problem from everything above, and a bigger one: it needs either a reader
who can key continuous Serto at speed, or a Serto HTR model good enough to draft it for
correction. It is listed here for completeness and because someone reading this page may know
that a usable model already exists, which would be worth more to us than the pages themselves.

## And the seat itself

Stated plainly, because it is the honest version of all six items: this project would like a
Syriacist to take the empty seat — not to audit everything, but to be the person a genuinely
undecidable reading gets sent to, a few times a year.

## What happens to an answer

An answer goes into the record it belongs to, with the reasoning, and the reading's status
moves from proposed to adjudicated. Any lesson built on that record is rebuilt from it.
Contributions are credited by name in the record and on this page unless you would rather they
were not, and everything here — records, lessons, drills — stays free, without an account or a
paywall, permanently.

## The licence facts, since you will check them

- **The four printed sources are public domain** — Nestle 1889, Payne Smith 1903, Nöldeke 1904,
  Barnes 1914 — and no licence is claimed over their text. A transcription of a public-domain

- **What this project offers under [CC BY-NC 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/)**
  is its own work: the lesson prose, the drills, the glosses and notes, and the encoding as a
  compilation.
- **One conflict is unresolved, and it is on the manuscript side, not this one.** The ground
  truth behind the hand track — Cod. Syr. 1, from the HTR Winter School Vienna — ships a
  `LICENSE.md` containing the full text of **CC BY-SA 4.0** beside an `htr-united.yml`
  declaring **CC BY 4.0**. Both sites state BY-SA, which is the conservative reading and is
  true under either. It has not been settled with the source. If you know who at the Winter
  School can answer that, saying so would itself be a help.
